1. What is water pollution?

Water pollution is the contamination of rivers, lakes, groundwater and seas by waste, chemicals, heat or disease-causing microbes. It makes water unsafe for drinking, farming, industry and aquatic life.

  • Surface water: rivers, lakes, ponds.
  • Groundwater: wells and aquifers, slow to recover once polluted.
  • Marine: coasts and seas, often from river discharge.
  • Thermal: hot water released by industries.

In India the challenge is large because of a dense population, fast-growing cities, industry and intensive farming.

Water pollution in India ↗ · Types of water pollution ↗

Point source: one clear place, such as a factory pipe or drain. Non-point source: many places, such as farm runoff or street wash. In India, a large population, fast urban growth, industry and farming all put pressure on rivers and groundwater.

2. Main sources

Point sources have a clear outlet (a drain or pipe). Non-point sources are spread over an area (field runoff).

  • Domestic sewage: untreated or partly treated waste from towns and villages.
  • Industrial effluent: chemicals, heavy metals, dyes, oils, hot water.
  • Agricultural runoff: fertilizers, pesticides and soil washed by rain.
  • Solid waste and plastics: dumped in or near water bodies.
  • Others: mining, oil spills, religious offerings and tourism.

3. Parameters we measure

Click a sensor card on the dashboard to jump to its explanation.

pH

A scale of 0 to 14 showing how acidic or alkaline water is. Most natural freshwater sits near neutral. Sudden shifts often come from industrial discharge or chemical spills and stress fish and plants.

Dissolved oxygen (DO)

Oxygen dissolved in water, needed by fish and other life. Organic waste uses oxygen as bacteria break it down, so low DO is a strong sign of sewage or organic pollution. Cold, flowing water holds more oxygen.

Turbidity

Cloudiness caused by suspended silt, waste or algae, measured in NTU. High turbidity blocks sunlight, harms plants and can shelter germs, which makes disinfection harder.

Total dissolved solids (TDS)

The amount of dissolved salts and minerals, in mg/L. High TDS can come from industrial discharge, mining or runoff. It changes taste and makes water less suitable for drinking and irrigation.

Temperature

Warm water holds less oxygen and speeds up algae growth. Hot discharge from power plants or factories can stress aquatic life.

Also used officially: BOD, COD, conductivity, nutrients, heavy metals and faecal coliform. Water quality parameters ↗

4. Effects of water pollution
  • Health: diarrhoea, cholera, typhoid and exposure to toxic chemicals.
  • Ecosystem: fish deaths, loss of biodiversity, broken food chains.
  • Eutrophication: excess nutrients cause algae blooms and oxygen loss.
  • Economy: costlier water treatment, lower crop and fishery output.
  • Society: loss of safe water, mainly for poorer communities.

Effects of water pollution ↗

Chain effect: pollution enters the food chain through fish, crops and drinking water. It also raises water treatment cost and harms farming, fishing and tourism.

5. Digital technologies for monitoring
  • IoT sensors: probes in the water send pH, DO, turbidity, TDS and temperature over wireless links for near real-time data.
  • Remote sensing / satellites: track algae, turbidity and changes across whole river stretches.
  • AI / machine learning: spot unusual readings and forecast trends.
  • GIS and dashboards: maps and live views so authorities can act fast.
  • Mobile and citizen apps: public reporting and awareness.

Limits: sensor cost, calibration and cleaning, power and network gaps, and data accuracy.

6. Who monitors water in India
  • CPCB (Central Pollution Control Board) and State Pollution Control Boards monitor water quality and enforce rules.
  • The National Water Quality Monitoring Programme samples rivers and other water bodies.
  • Ministry of Jal Shakti and the National Mission for Clean Ganga (Namami Gange) work on river cleaning.
  • The Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974 is the main law.
  • Real-time monitoring stations exist on some rivers; check CPCB for current details.

7. Remediation (solutions)
  • Treat: sewage (STP) and effluent (ETP) treatment plants that actually run well.
  • Regulate: strict discharge limits, inspection and penalties.
  • Restore: wetlands, bioremediation and green riverbanks.
  • Reduce: better farming (less fertilizer, buffer strips) and less plastic.
  • Reuse: treated wastewater for industry and irrigation.
  • Involve people: awareness drives and citizen reporting.

8. Standards at a glance

Drinking water in India follows BIS IS 10500. Rivers are grouped by CPCB designated best use classes (A to E), from drinking source after disinfection to irrigation and cooling. See the "Standard values" table on the main page.

9. Key terms
  • BOD: biochemical oxygen demand, oxygen microbes need to break down waste.
  • COD: chemical oxygen demand, oxygen needed to chemically oxidise waste.
  • NTU: nephelometric turbidity unit, the unit of turbidity.
  • Effluent: liquid waste released by a factory or plant.
  • Eutrophication: nutrient overload causing algae blooms.
  • STP / ETP: sewage / effluent treatment plant.
